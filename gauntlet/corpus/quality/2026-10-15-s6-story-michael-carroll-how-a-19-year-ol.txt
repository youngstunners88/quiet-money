At 19, Michael Carroll won £9.7 million on the British lottery. Eight years later, he was completely broke and cutting meat in a slaughterhouse. In 2002, Carroll was working as a binman earning just £42 a week. Within weeks, he bought a five-bedroom mansion and turned the lawn into a demolition derby track. He bought fleet after fleet of brand-new luxury cars, only to wreck them for fun. But here's the part nobody mentions: the money didn't vanish on reckless driving. It vanished because sudden wealth acts like an accelerant when capital arrives before financial discipline. Without social boundaries, he handed out £4 million to friends and hangers-on who quickly disappeared. By 2010, the parties stopped, the mansion was condemned, and his bank account hit zero. Money doesn't change who you are; it just amplifies every single flaw you already have. When capital outpaces emotional maturity, the velocity of money always works in reverse. The rule: never increase your lifestyle spending until your baseline capital is locked in income-producing assets. Follow for the money rules school never taught you, before easy money leaves you empty-handed.
