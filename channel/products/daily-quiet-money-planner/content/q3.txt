# WEEK 27 | Investing basics | tree,bars,gauge
L | Owning versus lending | A stock is a small piece of ownership in a company. A bond is a loan to a company or a government that pays interest. Cash is stable but earns little. Most portfolios are a mix of the three. This book is education, not a recommendation to buy anything. | Write one sentence each, in your own words, explaining what a stock, a bond and cash do.
N | Why not just cash? | $10,000 left in a drawer for 20 years at 3% inflation has the buying power of about {infl:10000,3,20}. The same $10,000 at a hypothetical 7% a year becomes {lump:10000,7,20}. Returns are not guaranteed and investments can fall in value. | Write what you hold in cash today. Write how many years you will not need it.
D | Time horizon first | How long until you need the money matters more than any product. Money you need in two years has a different job from money you will not touch for twenty. | Write three goals with the year you need the money for each one.
M | Investing is not gambling | Gambling has a negative expected value and ends with each bet. Long-term investing in broad markets owns real businesses that produce goods and profits. But returns still vary and losses happen. | Write one difference between a bet and a long-term investment in your own words.
A | Open the window | Look at how one investment account works where you live. Find out the fees, the minimums and the tax rules. Write down three questions to ask. | Write three questions to ask before opening or funding an investment account.

# WEEK 28 | Funds and costs | stack,curve,ring
L | Do not pick one stock | A fund holds many investments in one package. An index fund copies a market index, like the largest companies in a country, instead of trying to pick winners. That spreads risk across many companies at a low cost. | Write what a fund is and how an index fund differs from picking individual stocks.
N | The cost of picking | If a fund costs 1% a year and an index fund costs 0.05%, over 30 years on $100,000 at a gross 7% the gap is {gap:100000,6.95,6,30}. Costs are one of the few things you can control. | Find the cost percentage of any fund you own or are considering.
D | Read the fund sheet | Every fund publishes a fact sheet with its fees, holdings and past performance. Past performance does not predict the future. | Download one fund fact sheet. Write the expense ratio and the top three holdings.
M | The star manager story | Studies of fund performance have repeatedly found that most actively managed funds do not beat their benchmark index over long periods, after fees. That is a pattern in data, not a promise. | Write why a story about a star investor is more exciting than a boring index.
A | Diversification check | Diversification means not putting everything in one place. Count how many different companies, countries and asset types your investments cover. | Look at your own holdings and write how concentrated they are.
S | Weekly check-in: did you learn something about costs this week that changes any decision?
R | Do you prefer a simple plan you can stick to or a clever plan you will drop?

# WEEK 30 | Risk and ups and downs | gauge,curve,scale
L | Volatility is the price | Investments that can grow can also fall. Volatility is how much the value jumps around. It is the price of long-term growth, and it is why you do not invest money you will need soon. | Draw a zigzag line that trends upward. Write beside it what each drop would feel like.
N | Big drops happen | Stock markets have fallen by more than half in the past: US stocks dropped by more than 50% between 2007 and 2009. A $10,000 investment that falls 50% loses {usd:10000*0.5} and has {usd:10000*0.5} left. It then takes a 100% gain just to get back to even. | Write how much a 50% drop on your own investments would be in dollars.
D | Know your risk comfort | Risk tolerance is how much loss you can live with without selling in a panic. Imagine your investments down 30% for a year. What would you do? Write the truth. | Write your honest reaction to a 30% fall. Do you buy, hold or sell?
M | Recency bias | We overweight what happened recently. After good years, people think good years last. After a crash, they think it will not end. Both are guesses dressed as facts. | Write one belief about markets that is based only on the last few years.
A | A written plan | A one-page plan with your goals, your mix of investments and what you will do in a crash stops you from improvising under stress. | Write a three-line plan: what I own, how often I add, what I do when it falls.
S | Weekly check-in: how did you react to any market news this week?
R | What helps you stay calm when things are uncertain?

# WEEK 31 | Active and passive income | road,tree,pair
L | Time for money, or money for money | Active income comes from your time and work. Passive income comes from assets you own, such as investments, rental property or a business that runs without you. Passive rarely starts passive: it usually takes capital or years of work to build. | Draw two columns: Active and Passive. List every income source you have in the right one.
N | The passive number | A common rule of thumb says a portfolio can pay out about 4% a year. At that rate, {usd:2000} a month of passive income needs about {cap:2000} invested. That 4% figure comes from US research done in the 1990s and is a guideline, not a guarantee. | Write the monthly amount you would want from passive income. Multiply it by 12, then divide by 0.04.
D | Build the road | Investing {usd:1000} a month at a hypothetical 8% a year would take about {mtt:1000,8,2000*12/0.04} years to reach that amount. Change the monthly amount or the target and the date moves. | Write your target and how long it would take at the monthly amount you could invest.
M | Passive is a slogan | Many online promises call income passive when it is really work you do first. That does not make it bad. It only means plan for the work, not for a miracle. | Write one income stream you have seen advertised as passive and what work it might hide.
A | Income audit | Add up each source of income and mark how many hours it takes. Dollars per hour is a useful honest number. | Write each source's monthly income and weekly hours. Divide.
S | Weekly check-in: which of your income streams gave the most money per hour this week?

# WEEK 34 | The value equation | scale,steps,curve
L | Why some work pays more | One way to think about value: the bigger the result a customer wants and the more they believe you can deliver, the more they will pay. The less time and effort it takes them, the more they will pay. Raise the top, shrink the bottom. | Write the dream outcome your work gives someone, in one sentence.
N | Hourly to value | A freelancer charging $30 an hour for 10 hours earns {x:30,10}. A fixed price of $500 for the same result also earns $500 but shifts the focus from time to outcome. If they get faster, the effective rate on 5 hours is {div:500,5} an hour. | Write how you could price one of your skills by result instead of by hour.
D | Shrink the wait | People pay more for speed and simplicity. Look at what makes your work slow or hard for the customer and fix one thing. | Write one step that makes your service slow or confusing for the customer.
M | Proof builds belief | People believe what they can see: examples, reviews, before-and-after, a clear guarantee you can honour. Never invent proof. | Write one honest piece of proof you could collect this month.
A | Learn a skill | Skills that solve costly problems tend to pay more. Pick one skill that would raise your value and give it 30 minutes. | Schedule 30 minutes for the next three days on one skill.
S | Weekly check-in: what did you improve about your skill or your offer this week?

# WEEK 35 | Assets and liabilities | pair,scale,stack
L | The pocket test | One simple definition says an asset puts money in your pocket each month, and a liability takes money out. By that test, a home you live in is a liability while it costs you money each month, even if its value may rise. Not everyone defines it this way. | Sort everything you own into two columns: puts money in my pocket, takes money out.
N | Cash flow, not value | A rental that earns $1,500 a month and costs $1,200 in loan, tax, repairs and insurance has a positive cash flow of {usd:1500-1200} a month. A car that costs $450 a month is a negative cash flow of {usd:450}. Cash flow is what your monthly bank balance feels. | Write the monthly cash flow of one thing you own, positive or negative.
D | The expense trap | The rat race is a cycle: earn, spend more as you earn more, and need the next paycheck. You step out when your passive income covers your monthly expenses. | Write your monthly expenses and your current passive income. Divide the second by the first.
M | Good and bad debt | Debt that buys something that pays you can make sense. Debt that buys something that only costs you rarely does. Both carry risk. The line is thinner than it looks. | Write one debt you have and decide honestly which side it is on.
A | One asset step | Choose one small step toward owning something that pays you: an index fund purchase, a skill course, a first customer. | Write the step and the date you will take it.
S | Weekly check-in: did any money go to something that pays you instead of something that costs you?
R | What does being free of the paycheck cycle mean to you?

# WEEK 37 | Leverage cuts both ways | scale,gauge,steps
L | Borrowed money magnifies | Leverage means using borrowed money to control a larger asset. If the asset rises, your gain is multiplied. If it falls, your loss is too. It can build wealth and it can also wipe it out. | Write what leverage means in your own words, in one sentence.
N | The 5x house | A $100,000 property bought with $20,000 down is {num:100000/20000}x leverage. A 20% price rise adds {x:100000,0.2} to your value, which doubles your $20,000. A 20% fall removes the same amount and wipes out your whole down payment, before costs and interest. | Write one leveraged purchase you have made or might make and what a 10% drop would do.
D | Margin for error | Planners suggest keeping a cushion: cash reserves, a fixed-rate loan and payments you could afford even if income dropped. Write your own margin. | Write what would have to go wrong for a loan you hold to become unaffordable.
M | Survivorship | We hear from the people whose leveraged bets worked. We do not hear from those who lost everything. The silent group is large. | Write a story you have heard where debt worked out and ask: what are the silent cases?
A | Stress test | Take a loan you hold. What if your income dropped 20% for six months? Could you cover it? | Write the answer and one change that would make it safer.
S | Weekly check-in: did any borrowing decision come up this week? What did you ask before you decided?
R | Which matters more to you: faster growth or fewer sleepless nights?

# WEEK 38 | Taxes, in brief | ring,scale,shield
L | Marginal and average | In many countries, income is taxed in bands: each band has its own rate. Your marginal rate applies only to the last dollars you earn, not all of them. Rules differ by country, so check your own official source. | Look up your country's tax bands from an official government site. Write them here.
N | An example | Suppose the first $10,000 is taxed at 10% and everything above it at 20%. On $30,000 of income that is {usd:10000*0.1+20000*0.2} of tax, an average rate of {pct:10000*0.1+20000*0.2,30000}, not 20%. This is a made-up example, not any real tax system. | Use the example to calculate the tax on $50,000.
D | Know what is deductible | Tax rules can let you reduce what you owe through allowances, credits or contributions to some accounts. This is general information, not tax advice. | Write two ways to lower tax that your official source says are available to you.
M | Raise myths | Many people fear a raise will push them into a bracket and reduce their take-home pay. In a banded system, only the extra income is taxed at the higher rate, so a raise should still raise your pay. | Write one tax belief you have and check it against an official source.
A | Save your records | Receipts, statements and a simple folder make tax time shorter. A shoebox is a start. | Create one folder, paper or digital, for this year's tax records.
S | Weekly check-in: what did you do this week to make the next tax season easier?
